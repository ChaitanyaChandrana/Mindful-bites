Why it matters

Your eight daily checks, and why

  • Potassium and phosphorus within your limits. Both build up when kidney function is low, and high potassium is the one that can be dangerous. The limits are the ones saved in Health, so your care team can change them.
  • Protein within your range. Lower protein intake in advanced kidney disease reduces waste build-up and phosphorus load. Staying too low causes muscle loss, so the app shows a range.
  • Sodium within limit. Sodium raises blood pressure and fluid load. The count includes sodium bicarbonate once you tick it.
  • Acid load at or below +10. Lower dietary acid is linked to slower kidney decline in observational studies. It is an estimate.
  • Medicines taken. Sevelamer only works with meals, and bicarbonate and blood pressure medicines only help if taken.
  • Moved 30+ minutes. General guidance for kidney disease is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week.
  • No NSAIDs. Ibuprofen and naproxen can harm kidneys at advanced stages, so guidelines advise avoiding them.
  • A good day is 3 meals logged, potassium in range and 6 of 8 checks. This scoring is the app's own design for staying motivated, not a clinical measure, and you get one rest day in any seven so a bad day does not erase your progress.

Water and fluids

  • No guideline gives one water number for stage 4–5 without dialysis. The KDIGO 2024 text I could read has none. The National Kidney Foundation says people in stage 4–5 usually need to limit fluids, with a rule of thumb of about as much as the urine you pass. One expert review argues the opposite: enough water to pass 2–3 litres of urine. They disagree, and the answer depends on your urine output, swelling, heart and blood sodium. So the app asks you for the range your nephrologist sets.
  • The biggest trial (CKD WIT, 631 people with stage 3 disease) coached people to drink 1–1.5 litres extra for a year. Kidney decline did not change, and a few people’s sodium dipped low. That was stage 3, not eGFR 13, so it cannot be applied to you directly.
  • Too little matters more with Farxiga: its label warns of volume depletion and sudden kidney injury, especially when eGFR is low. Too much can cause swelling and low blood sodium, which is fairly common in advanced kidney disease.
  • Salt makes you thirsty and holds water, and sodium bicarbonate carries sodium. Weighing yourself each morning is the simplest check on extra fluid.
  • Sick-day rule: with vomiting, diarrhoea or fever, UK kidney guidance says to pause medicines such as SGLT2 inhibitors (Farxiga), ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics and NSAIDs until you are eating and drinking normally. Ask your care team first.
  • Exercise: weigh before and after a gym session. Each kilogram lost is roughly one litre of sweat (basic physiology, not a clinical rule). Plain water carries no potassium; coconut water has about 600 mg a cup.
  • Hydration and your blood tests: one small study in healthy women found a glass of water did not change creatinine. I found no study on potassium or phosphate.

Walking and exercise

  • Trials in kidney disease show exercise improves fitness, walking ability, blood pressure and quality of life. KDIGO 2024 suggests about 150 minutes a week of moderate activity as tolerated, with some strength work.
  • A 2019 pooled analysis of 13 trials in people not on dialysis found a small gain in eGFR (about 2.6) and systolic blood pressure about 5.6 mmHg lower. Those people were mostly stage 3 to 4. At your stage there is almost no trial data, so treat walking as protecting your heart, muscles and mood, not as a promise to lift eGFR.
  • Creatinine and exercise. Hard exercise temporarily changes creatinine and cystatin C, and creatinine-based eGFR estimates fell roughly 16 to 30% right after very intense sessions in studies. More muscle also means a higher creatinine without kidney damage. Many clinicians suggest a rest day before blood tests (a common habit, not a formal rule). If you lift heavy, ask whether a cystatin C test can sit beside your creatinine.
  • Exercise can nudge potassium up for a short time. With potassium around 5.5, keep sessions moderate, drink as advised, and check with your nephrologist about your gym intensity.

Sevelamer and phosphorus

  • Sevelamer works inside the gut. It binds phosphorus from the meal you eat with it, so it must be taken with food. It does not remove phosphorus already in your blood.
  • No trial reports milligrams bound per tablet. A reasonable estimate is about 40 mg of phosphorus per 800 mg tablet, within a range of roughly 20 to 65. Trials show blood phosphorus falling about 1 to 2 mg/dL on 2.4 to 7.2 g a day.
  • Phosphorus added to packaged food is almost fully absorbed (near 100%), animal foods about 40 to 60%, and plant foods about 20 to 50%. A binder removes a roughly fixed amount, so it cannot cancel a high-phosphorus meal.
  • Skipping the meal usually means skipping the dose. The label spaces some medicines away from it (for example ciprofloxacin, mycophenolate, levothyroxine), so ask your pharmacist about your list. Studies have not shown that binders improve survival before dialysis. They are used to keep blood phosphorus in range.

Fruit and acid load

  • Fruit and vegetables push the acid score below zero mostly because of their potassium. Studies in stage 3 to 4 found a fruit and vegetable diet lowered markers of kidney injury, but those patients started with potassium at or below 4.6.
  • With potassium around 5.5, high-potassium picks such as banana, orange, coconut water, potato, tomato and dried fruit are risky even though they look alkaline. Lower-potassium choices are apple, berries, grapes, pineapple, pear, cabbage and cauliflower.
  • You also take sodium bicarbonate. The lab that matters is your blood bicarbonate, and a more negative score is not always better. Your nephrologist decides on any change.

How foods are rated
  • Safe per serving: potassium under 150 mg, phosphorus under 100 mg, sodium under 150 mg.
  • Limit: potassium 150–250, phosphorus 100–200, sodium 150–400, or over 15 g protein. Keep to the portion shown.
  • Avoid: anything above those, plus foods that are never safe for you.
  • Plant phosphorus counts half, since the body absorbs less of it. Sprouted or fermented grains don't get this discount.
Never: star fruit, coconut water, low-sodium or "lite" salt (potassium chloride), fruit juice, banana, mango, dry fruits, and packaged food with "phosphate" or E338–E343 / E450–E452 on the label.
Double-boil vegetables and dal: cut small, boil in plenty of water, drain, boil again in fresh water, then do the tadka. Pressure-cooking and steaming don't remove potassium. Soaking alone barely does.
